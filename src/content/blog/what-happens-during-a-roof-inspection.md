Most homeowners have never watched a roof inspection actually happen. That
makes it easy for a bad one to hide — ten minutes, a glance from a ladder, a
verbal "yeah, you've got some damage." Here's what a real inspection looks
like, top to bottom, and how to judge whether the one you're getting is worth
anything.

## On the roof: what gets photographed and why

### Every slope, individually

Damage is directional. Hail and wind hit some slopes and spare others, so each
slope gets its own overview shot plus close-ups of anything notable. This
matters enormously on insurance claims — an adjuster scopes by slope, and
slope-labeled photos are what let your documentation line up with their
paperwork. See our [Woodlands storm damage page](/the-woodlands/storm-damage)
for how that packet feeds a claim.

### Valleys, penetrations, and flashing

Valleys carry the most water on the roof, so they fail first and get inspected
hardest. Then every penetration: pipe boots (the neoprene collars around
plumbing vents are one of the most common causes of roof leaks — [we wrote a
whole post on them](/blog/why-roofs-leak-around-pipe-boots)), furnace and vent
stacks, satellite mounts, and the flashing at chimneys, walls, and skylights.
A surprising share of "my roof is leaking" calls trace to a $10 rubber collar
or a lifted piece of step flashing, not the shingles at all.

### Shingle condition: granules, seals, nail pops

- **Granule loss.** Granules aren't cosmetic — they're the shingle's UV shield.
  IBHS's peer-reviewed research (Frontiers in Materials, 2025) shows that once
  granules are displaced, the exposed asphalt embrittles; their testing found
  repeated sub-severe hail (0.7–1.0") plus two years of weathering left
  shingles performing like decade-old material. We look for granule
  accumulation in gutters and bare or shiny spots on the mat.
- **Seal integrity.** IBHS's wind research identifies seal strength as _the_
  key factor in wind performance, and it declines as shingles age. We check for
  unsealed tabs by hand along edges and ridges.
- **Wind evidence.** Per IIBEC, uplift that creases or tears a shingle is wind
  damage — a crease line across a tab is the signature we photograph.
- **Hail evidence.** Bruised mats, displaced granules with a fresh impact
  pattern, and matching strikes on soft metals (vents, flashing, gutters) that
  date the event.
- **Nail pops and mechanical issues.** Backed-out fasteners telegraph through
  shingles and open small entry points; they're cheap to fix now and expensive
  to ignore.

## In the attic: the half most inspections skip

A roof inspection that never opens the attic hatch is half an inspection.
From below we check:

- **Daylight and water staining** at penetrations and along the decking —
  active leaks show here before ceilings do.
- **Decking condition** — soft spots, delamination, and the musty smell of a
  moisture problem.
- **Ventilation.** The Asphalt Roofing Manufacturers Association notes a
  poorly vented attic can hit **140°F on a 90°F day**, and that heat-plus-
  moisture combination deteriorates decking and shingles from underneath. The
  benchmark is the 1-in-300 rule: one square foot of net free ventilation area
  per 300 square feet of attic (the code base ratio is 1/150), split between
  high exhaust and soffit intake, with intake at least equal to exhaust (ARMA
  and GAF Technical Bulletin R-120). A roof that "aged fast" often turns out
  to have been cooking from below.

## When to get one

- **After a storm.** Hail and wind damage is often invisible from the ground.
  If a storm just hit, start with our
  [storm-damage checklist](/blog/storm-damage-what-to-do), then get the
  documented inspection before you decide whether to file.
- **Buying a home.** A general home inspection covers the roof briefly; a
  roof-specific inspection tells you what the next decade costs. Agents: a
  24-hour written report with photos is built for an option period — it either
  clears the roof or gives your buyer a concrete repair credit number.
- **Selling a home.** A clean pre-listing roof report removes the single
  scariest unknown from a buyer's inspection response.
- **Every couple of years, and as the roof ages.** Small findings — a split
  pipe boot, a few unsealed tabs, a clogged ridge vent — are repair-priced
  problems that become replacement-priced problems when they run for years.
  Our [roof repair page](/the-woodlands/roof-repair) covers that end of the
  spectrum.

## The ten-minute test

If an inspector is on and off your roof in ten minutes with no photos, no
attic check, and no written report — you didn't get an inspection, you got a
sales call. Ask any roofer three questions first: _How many photos will I get?
Do you check the attic? Is the report written?_
