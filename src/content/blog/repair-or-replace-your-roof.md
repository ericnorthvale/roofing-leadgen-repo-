Every roofer you call has an incentive on this question, and you know it. A
replacement is worth ten to twenty times a repair.

So here is the decision made out loud — the four things that actually settle
it, and the honest answer on each.

## Question 1: how old is it?

This is the first filter, and it does most of the work.

InterNACHI puts 3-tab asphalt at around 20 years and architectural at around
30 — **less in a hot climate like ours.** Sustained heat, intense UV and
humidity dry out the asphalt binder, which is why a Houston-area roof rarely
reaches the number printed on the wrapper.

- **Under about 10 years:** repair, almost always. A roof this age with a
  localised problem has a lot of life left and a patch is honest work.
- **Roughly 10 to 18 years:** it depends on everything below.
- **Past about 20 years:** a repair is often money spent on a roof you will
  replace within a couple of years anyway. We will still do it if you need to
  buy time — just with your eyes open about what it is.

## Question 2: is the problem in one place, or everywhere?

A roof fails either at a _detail_ or across a _field_. Those are different
situations.

**Detail failures are repairs.** A cracked pipe boot, failed step flashing at a
wall, a debris-packed valley, a handful of lifted ridge caps, nail pops. These
are specific, findable, fixable — and they cause the large majority of leaks we
get called to. The neoprene collar on a plumbing vent commonly cracks within
roughly 10 to 15 years of UV exposure and is behind more mystery ceiling stains
than anything else on a roof.

**Field failures are replacements.** Widespread granule loss, brittleness and
cracking across whole slopes, wind creasing over large areas, hail bruising
scattered across every elevation. The common thread is that no amount of
patching catches up, because the material itself is finished.

The test: if we can draw a circle around it, we can repair it.

## Question 3: what does the decking look like?

This is the question that changes answers, and nobody can answer it from the
ground.

Tile, shingles and underlayment all hide the plywood beneath them. A roof can
look serviceable and be sitting on decking that has been quietly rotting around
a long-term leak for years. The photo at the top of this article is from a
Woodlands job — that is what we found when the shingles came off.

When we find that, the conversation changes. Rotted decking is not a repair, it
is structure, and patching a roof over soft plywood is putting new material on
a bad foundation.

It also means **a replacement quote that doesn't mention decking is incomplete.**
Nobody knows how much needs replacing until the tear-off, so an honest estimate
tells you the per-sheet price up front rather than surprising you with it on day
two.

## Question 4: was a storm involved?

This one is less about the roof and more about who pays.

If a covered peril caused the damage — wind, hail, a falling limb — your
insurance may cover a replacement that you'd otherwise be paying for in full.
That changes the arithmetic completely, and it is worth establishing _before_
you commit to a repair.

Damage from a storm also tends to be the kind that argues for replacement
anyway: hail bruising and wind creasing are field failures, not detail
failures. [What hail damage actually looks like](/blog/what-hail-damage-looks-like-on-a-roof/)
walks through how to tell.

## What a repair actually costs

For market context, Angi (2026) puts typical roof repairs at **$400 to $2,000**
nationally, against a national average roof replacement around **$9,602** with
a full span of $5,900 to $46,000. Those are national figures, not our quotes —
[what a replacement costs here](/blog/roof-replacement-cost-the-woodlands/) has
the detail with sources attached.

The ratio is the point. A repair is roughly 5% of a replacement. Which means a
contractor who skips straight to replacement without showing you why has made a
very expensive recommendation on your behalf.
