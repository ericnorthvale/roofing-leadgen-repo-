A roof is one of the largest unplanned purchases a household makes, and it
rarely arrives at a convenient moment. Nobody saves up for a roof. A storm
comes through, or a ceiling stain appears, and suddenly there's a five-figure
decision with a deadline attached.

There are five real ways people pay for it. Each suits a different situation,
and the wrong one is usually chosen because it was the only one anybody
mentioned.

Here they are honestly, including where each one is a bad idea.

## 1. Insurance — if a covered peril caused it

Always the first question, because it changes the arithmetic completely rather
than just spreading it out.

If wind, hail or a falling limb caused the damage, your policy may pay for a
replacement you would otherwise fund yourself. It is worth establishing
**before** you commit to paying out of pocket or to a repair.

Two things people get wrong here:

**The first cheque is not the whole settlement.** A replacement-cost policy
pays actual cash value first and holds back recoverable depreciation until the
work is done and invoiced.
[What happens if your insurance doesn't cover your entire roof](/blog/insurance-wont-cover-entire-roof/)
walks through the split — and the back half that a lot of homeowners never
claim.

**Insurance doesn't pay for age.** A roof that is simply worn out is not a
claim, and filing one you can't win costs you time and leaves a record. If what
we find won't clear your deductible, we will say so.

If a storm was involved, start with
[what hail damage actually looks like](/blog/what-hail-damage-looks-like-on-a-roof/)
rather than a door-knocker's opinion.

## 2. Cash

The cheapest option, obviously — no interest, no application, no lien.

The thing worth saying out loud: paying cash does not oblige you to take the
cheapest scope. The most common cash mistake we see is a homeowner stripping
line items out of a quote to hit a number — reusing pipe boots, skipping
ventilation work, leaving the decking contingency out — and buying a new roof
with an old roof's problems built into it.

If the gap between what you have and what the roof needs is small, it is
usually better to borrow the difference than to delete the parts you can't see.

## 3. Home equity — a HELOC or home-equity loan

For larger projects this is where many Texas homeowners land, and there are
**state-specific rules that don't apply elsewhere.**

The Texas Constitution (article XVI, §50(a)(6)(B)) caps total debt secured
against a homestead at **80% of its fair market value**, and home-equity loans
here are limited to your **primary residence**. So a rental or second home does
not qualify, and if you're already highly leveraged the maths may rule it out
regardless of your credit.

The trade-off is plain: these are usually the lowest-cost borrowing available
because your house is the collateral — which is also precisely the risk. A roof
is a sensible thing to secure against a house; it protects the asset. But a
missed payment has a consequence an unsecured loan doesn't.

## 4. An unsecured home-improvement or personal loan

A fixed-rate loan with no lien on your home, no appraisal and no equity
requirement.

It typically costs more than home equity, and it usually asks for stronger
credit. In exchange it's faster, your house stays out of it, and it doesn't
touch the 80% homestead cap — which matters if you're near it or if the roof
is on a property that isn't your primary residence.

This is often the right answer for a mid-sized job on a household with good
credit that doesn't want a lien over a roof.

## 5. Contractor-arranged financing

Many roofers, us included, point customers to lenders. Used well it's
convenient. There are two things to watch.

**Know whether your contractor is paid for the referral.** Some are. We are
not — our [financing page](/financing) lists options as a convenience, not an
endorsement, and we take nothing either way. Ask any roofer the same question
directly.

**"No money down" is not the same as "cheaper."** It's a payment structure, not
a discount. Compare the total cost of the work, not the size of the first
payment.

## The trap: anyone who offers to cover your deductible

This one isn't a judgement call, it's the law.

Under **Texas Insurance Code §707.002** the insured _shall_ pay the deductible,
and **Business &amp; Commerce Code §27.02** makes it an offence for a
contractor to pay, rebate or absorb it. The Texas Department of Insurance notes
it carries up to a $2,000 fine and up to six months in jail. Insurers may also
require proof that you paid your deductible before releasing your recoverable
depreciation — so the "favour" can cost you the back half of your settlement
too.

It is presented as a kindness. It is a crime with your name on it as well as
theirs. The Department of Insurance also warns that after a disaster it is
illegal for an out-of-town contractor to take a down payment before starting
work, and that you should never sign a contract assigning work "for the value
of insurance proceeds."

## Decide the scope first, then the funding

The most expensive mistake isn't picking the wrong loan. It's letting the
funding method quietly decide the roof.

Get the work defined first — measured, itemised, with the per-sheet decking
rate named up front, which is
[what a roofing estimate should include](/blog/what-should-a-roofing-estimate-include/).
Then work out how to pay for it. Done in that order, the comparison between
funding routes is simple arithmetic. Done backwards, you end up with whatever
roof fits the cheque, and the parts that got removed are invariably the ones
you'd never see.

It is also worth knowing whether you need a full replacement at all —
[repair or replace](/blog/repair-or-replace-your-roof/) sets out the four
questions that settle it, and a repair is roughly 5% of a replacement.
