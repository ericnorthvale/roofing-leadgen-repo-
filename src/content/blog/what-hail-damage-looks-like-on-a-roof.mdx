Hail comes through, the neighbours get new roofs, and you stand in the driveway
squinting at yours wondering whether anything actually happened.

This is the most common question we get after a storm, and the honest answer is
that **real hail damage is hard to see from the ground and easy to confuse with
ordinary ageing.** Here is what it actually looks like, what an adjuster is
checking for, and why the thing most homeowners point at usually isn't it.

## The three things hail does to an asphalt shingle

The Insurance Institute for Business & Home Safety tests shingles by firing
laboratory hailstones at them — 1.5 inch and 2 inch — and scores three separate
kinds of damage: **dents, tears, and granule loss.** Those are the three things
anyone assessing your roof is looking for too.

### 1. Bruising, and the soft spot

This is the one that matters most and the one you cannot see.

A hailstone big enough can fracture the fibreglass mat inside the shingle
without breaking the surface. The shingle looks more or less fine. Press on the
impact point and it gives — soft, slightly spongy, like pressing a bruise on an
apple. That is a **mat fracture**, and it means the shingle's structure is
broken even though it hasn't leaked yet.

It is also why a roof can be genuinely destroyed and still look acceptable from
the street.

### 2. Granule loss — but the pattern, not the amount

Granules are the shingle's UV armour. Knock enough of them off and the asphalt
underneath starts cooking.

Here is the part nobody explains: **there is no magic number.** IBHS's own
protocol says plainly that there are no set criteria for how much granule loss
counts as damage. What matters is the _pattern_. Haag Engineering — whose
methodology much of the insurance industry works from — treats it as hail
damage when the impact has scuffed granules away enough to expose substantial
asphalt beneath.

So a patch of bare asphalt the size of a coin, with a dent in the middle of it,
is damage. A general thinning across a whole slope is a roof getting old.

### 3. Tears and cracking

Less common on modern laminated shingles, more common on older or brittle ones.
Visible splits radiating from an impact point.

## The test that actually separates hail from ageing

This is the single most useful thing in this article.

**Hail damage is random and circular. Ageing is even and directional.**

Hail falls where it falls. The impacts are scattered with no pattern, roughly
round, and they hit the horizontal surfaces hardest — which is why we check the
soft metals first.

Wear is the opposite. It is uniform across a slope, heaviest on the south and
west faces that take the most sun, and it follows the shingle's edges and
weather exposure rather than landing in random circles.

If what you're looking at is spread evenly across the whole roof, it is almost
certainly not hail, whatever the man who knocked on your door said.

## Check the soft metals first

Before anyone gets on the roof, walk around the house and look at the things
that dent easily:

- **Gutters and downspouts** — dents and dimples
- **Window screens** — small punctures or stretched mesh
- **Air-conditioner fins** — flattened in a scatter pattern
- **Vents, flashing and any painted metal** — bright spots where paint chipped
- **Fence caps, mailbox, grill lid** — anything soft and horizontal

This matters more than it sounds. Soft metals record hail honestly. If there's
nothing on any of them, it's unlikely a stone big enough to fracture a shingle
mat landed on your address. If they're covered, that's strong evidence a hail
event of a given size actually occurred _here_ — not three miles away where the
reports came from.

## The sub-severe hail problem

Here's something that gets missed, and it matters in a market that gets hit
repeatedly.

Hail too small to damage a shingle today still ages it. Research into
**sub-severe hail** — roughly three-quarters of an inch to an inch — found that
these smaller impacts, combined with ordinary weathering, leave shingles **more
vulnerable to a later severe storm.**

Practically: a roof that survived three small hail events is not the same roof
it was before them, even though no single storm "damaged" it. That is part of
why two houses on the same street can come out of one hailstorm with completely
different outcomes.
