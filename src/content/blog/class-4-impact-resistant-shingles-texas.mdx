Every roofer in the Houston hail belt will offer you a Class 4 upgrade, and most
will explain it the same way: hail-proof roof, big insurance discount. Neither
half of that is quite true.

Here is the honest version — what the rating actually measures, how much hail
this county actually gets, and how to find out what the upgrade is worth to
**you** rather than to a homeowner in a brochure.

## What "Class 4" actually means

Impact resistance is graded by a lab test called **UL 2218**, in which a steel
ball is dropped onto the shingle and it passes if the mat does not crack. Class
4 is the highest of the four grades, and it is earned by withstanding a
**2-inch steel ball dropped from 20 feet**.

It is a real, measurable standard rather than a marketing word. But read that
sentence again, because the detail that matters is buried in it: Class 4
describes **how a shingle behaved in a laboratory, against a steel ball**. It
is not a hail warranty, and no manufacturer offers it as one.

Two things fall out of that picture, and they pull in opposite directions.

**Hail lands here in most years.** Six years, six years with recorded events.
This is not a hypothetical risk being sold to you — stones fell on Pinehurst,
New Caney, Willis, Cut and Shoot, Montgomery, Shenandoah and Splendora, and
somebody reported each one.

**Every recorded stone was smaller than the test ball.** The largest on the
county record in those six years was 1.25 inches. The Class 4 test uses two.

A roofer trying to sell you the upgrade will show you the first fact. The
second one is why most of them will not show you this chart at all.

## So is it worth it, or not?

It still can be — but for different reasons than the pitch usually gives.

**The wrong reason** is "my roof will survive the next hailstorm." Nothing on
your roof is guaranteed to. Class 4 grades a steel ball in a laboratory, not a
storm over Spring — and the manufacturers are the ones saying so. IKO's own
wording on what the rating is for appears further down this page, and it is
blunter than anything a roofer is likely to tell you.

**The reasonable reasons** are these:

- **The insurance credit arrives every year**, in years with hail and years
  without. Over a roof's life that is the part of the maths that compounds.
- **Repeated small impacts still cost you granules.** One-inch hail rarely
  destroys a roof, but it does wear it, and bruising accumulates across the
  storms in that chart. A tougher mat ages better under that.
- **The materials are genuinely different.** IKO's Class 4 shingle, Nordic, is
  reinforced with polymer-modified (SBS) asphalt, which stays more flexible
  than standard asphalt. In a climate that puts roofs through Gulf heat as well
  as hail, flexibility is not only a hail property.

**When it is weaker:** if you expect to sell before the credit accumulates, or
if your carrier's credit turns out to be small. Which brings us to the part
nobody can answer for you.

## The insurance credit, and how to find out what yours is

Here is the sentence to be careful about, because a lot of Texas roofing sites
get it wrong: **no law requires your insurer to discount a Class 4 roof, and
nobody can tell you the percentage in advance.**

What the Texas Department of Insurance actually says is that a Class 4 roof
"would receive the highest premium credit" — the highest of the available
grades. But TDI is equally clear that **each company sets its own credit**.
Anyone quoting you a specific "20–35% saving" is quoting a number they cannot
know.

The good news is that finding out is genuinely easy, and takes one phone call:

1. **Ring your insurer** — the number on your policy, not an agent you found
   online — and ask: _"What premium credit do you give for a UL 2218 Class 4
   impact-resistant roof?"_
2. **Ask what proof they need.** Most want the manufacturer's certification;
   TDI's own verification uses **Form PC068**.
3. **Ask when it applies from** — the install date, or the next renewal.
4. **Get the number in writing**, then weigh it against the upgrade cost on
   your written estimate.

That is the whole calculation. With your real premium, your real credit and a
real quote for both options, the decision makes itself — and it will be the
right answer for your house rather than for an average one.

## What Class 4 does not do

Worth stating plainly, because it surprises people at claim time:

**Hail damage is not covered by the shingle warranty.** Not on Class 4, not on
anything. IKO puts it about as directly as a manufacturer can — the impact
rating, they say, "is solely for the purpose of enabling residential property
owners to obtain a reduction in their residential insurance premium, if
available."

Read that carefully. The manufacturer's own position is that the rating exists
to get you an insurance credit, **not** to promise the roof will survive hail.
Hail damage remains an insurance matter, which is why
[how your claim is paid](/blog/insurance-wont-cover-entire-roof/) matters more
to most homeowners than the shingle grade does.

## Which shingles are Class 4, and which only look like it

This is where the most expensive confusion happens, because two shingles from
the same manufacturer can sit on either side of the line.

| Shingle     | UL 2218 rating |
| ----------- | -------------- |
| IKO Nordic  | **Class 4**    |
| IKO Dynasty | Class 3        |

Dynasty is an excellent shingle and the one most homes here are re-roofed with.
It is **not** Class 4, and it will not earn the Class 4 credit — if someone has
quoted you Dynasty and mentioned an impact discount, that is worth a direct
question before you sign.
