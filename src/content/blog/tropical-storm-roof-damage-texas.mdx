A tropical system does something different to a roof than a hailstorm does, and
if you inspect for the wrong one you will miss it.

Hail is an impact event: discrete, random, circular. A tropical storm is a
**duration** event — many hours of sustained wind and driven rain working at
the same weak points over and over. The damage is concentrated where the roof
meets something else, and a lot of it is invisible from the ground.

## Wind doesn't attack the middle of a roof

It attacks the edges.

Air flowing over a roof creates uplift, and that uplift is strongest at the
perimeter — eaves, rakes, ridges, and the corners where two edges meet. That's
why wind damage reads so differently from hail damage.

**What to look for, in order:**

- **Ridge caps** — lifted, creased or missing. The ridge is the highest-uplift
  line on the roof and usually the first thing to go.
- **Rake and eave edges** — shingles curled back or starting lifted at the
  perimeter courses.
- **Creasing** — a horizontal crease across a shingle where wind folded it back
  and it dropped again. The sealant bond is broken even though the shingle is
  still in place. This is the one people miss entirely.
- **Missing tabs** — obvious, but note _where_: a scatter at the edges tells a
  different story from one gap mid-slope.

That creasing point matters. Research from the Insurance Institute for Business
& Home Safety has found that what actually fails in wind is the **sealant bond
between shingles**, and that bond weakens as a roof ages. A creased shingle
that stayed on the roof is a shingle that will leave on the next storm.

That third panel is the one worth memorising. Nothing is missing, nothing is
obviously broken, and the roof looks fine from the street — but the bond that
held that course down is gone, and the next storm does not have to work nearly
as hard.

## Wind-driven rain gets in where ordinary rain doesn't

This is the part that surprises people.

Normal rain falls downward and a roof handles it. Sustained horizontal rain
gets driven **up** under shingle courses, behind flashing, into ridge vent
openings and through soffits. A roof that is completely watertight in an
ordinary downpour can leak in a tropical system without anything being
"broken."

So the staining you find after a storm doesn't necessarily mean the roof
failed. It may mean the roof was asked to do something it was never designed
for. Worth knowing before you panic — and worth documenting either way.

## Then there is what the wind threw at it

Around here, the thing the wind throws is usually pine.

Limb strikes puncture, bruise and scrape. Check for impact points near trees,
and check the gutters for an unusual load of needles and bark, which tells you
how much came down. A branch that scuffed a line of granules off a slope has
aged that slope in a single afternoon.

## The policy question nobody mentions until it matters

Here is the one to check _before_ hurricane season rather than after.

Many Texas homeowner policies treat **named-storm or windstorm damage
separately from everything else** — commonly with a percentage deductible
rather than a flat dollar amount. A 2% deductible on a $400,000 home is $8,000,
not the $2,500 you might expect from your ordinary deductible.

Find your declarations page and look for a separate windstorm, hurricane or
named-storm deductible. It changes whether filing makes sense at all, and it is
a much better thing to discover in March than the week after landfall.

## What to do in the first 48 hours

1. **Document before anyone touches anything** — including before an emergency
   tarp goes on. Once a tarp is down, what your adjuster needs to see is
   underneath it.
2. **Stop active water** —
   [emergency tarping](/storm-response) is free and carries no obligation to
   use us for the repair.
3. **Photograph the collateral**, not just the roof: fence, screens, gutters,
   anything the wind moved. It establishes what conditions actually occurred at
   your address.
4. **Don't sign anything on your porch.** TDI's storm guidance is blunt about
   out-of-town contractors after a disaster, and you should never sign a
   contract assigning work "for the value of insurance proceeds."
5. **Get a written scope** before committing, not a handshake and a start date.

## Why this matters more on some roofs than others

Exposure is not the same everywhere we work. A roof on open water — the Lake
Conroe communities especially — takes sustained wind with nothing upwind to
break it, so uplift damage concentrates at edges and ridge caps far more than
on a sheltered inland lot. [Storm damage in Conroe](/conroe/storm-damage) goes
into that in more detail.

Under the pine canopy in The Woodlands the risk profile flips: less raw wind
exposure, far more debris.
