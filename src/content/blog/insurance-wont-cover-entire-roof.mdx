The claim was approved. The cheque arrived. It is nowhere near enough to pay
for the roof.

This is one of the most common calls we get, and it is almost never what it
looks like. In most cases nothing has gone wrong — the money is arriving in a
sequence nobody explained to you. In some cases something genuinely has gone
wrong, and there is a defined process for that too.

Here is how to tell which one you're in.

## First: your insurer probably hasn't short-paid you

If you have a **replacement cost** policy, the payout comes in two parts, and
the first part is deliberately smaller.

The insurer first pays **actual cash value** — what your roof was worth given
its age, with depreciation taken off. Then it holds the remainder, called
**recoverable depreciation**, until the work is actually finished and you prove
it. Your deductible comes off the first payment.

So the cheque in your hand is: your roof's depreciated value, minus your
deductible. Not the cost of the roof.

<ClaimPayoutDiagram class="my-10" />

The gold slice is what arrives up front. It is the smallest part of the total,
and on an older roof it is dramatically smaller — which is why a twenty-year-old
roof produces that stomach-drop moment when the envelope opens.

**The pale slice is not lost.** It is yours once the work is done and the
invoice goes in. A lot of homeowners never claim it, because nobody told them
it existed.

## The two-part sequence, in order

1. **Claim approved.** The insurer issues the first payment — ACV, minus your
   deductible.
2. **You have the work done.** The contractor invoices the full replacement
   cost.
3. **You submit the invoice and proof of payment.** The insurer releases the
   recoverable depreciation.

Step 3 is the one people miss. If you take the first cheque, do a cheap partial
repair and stop, you have left the depreciation on the table permanently.

Note also what the Texas Department of Insurance warns about cashing cheques:
some companies treat a signed settlement cheque as final. Read what you're
endorsing before you deposit it.

## Why your deductible is not negotiable — and why that's protection

Every so often a roofer offers to "cover your deductible" or "work with you" on
it. It sounds generous. It is a crime, and it names you as the other party.

Under **Texas Insurance Code §707.002** the insured _shall_ pay the deductible,
and **Business &amp; Commerce Code §27.02** makes it an offence for a
contractor to pay, rebate or absorb it. TDI notes it carries up to a $2,000
fine and up to six months in jail. Insurers may also require proof you paid
your deductible before they release the recoverable depreciation — so a roofer
who waives it can cost you the back half of your settlement as well.

If someone offers, that tells you what else they're willing to do on your roof.

## Now the real problem: partial approvals

Everything above is the system working. This is the system not working.

A **partial approval** is when the adjuster approves two slopes and not the
other two, or approves a repair where the roof needs replacing. You're left
holding an approval that cannot actually put your roof back.

There are a few distinct situations, and they have different answers.

### "Only the damaged slopes were approved"

Common after hail, which genuinely does hit some elevations harder than others.
Sometimes it's correct. Sometimes it's a scope that was written from the ground
or from one ladder position.

The counter is documentation, slope by slope. Adjusters scope by slope, so your
evidence has to be organised the same way — which is exactly why our inspection
packet photographs and labels each slope separately rather than giving one
verdict for the whole roof.

### "They approved a repair, but the shingle is discontinued"

This is the one most worth knowing about. If your shingle line is no longer
made, a repair cannot restore the roof to its previous condition — the patch
will not match, in colour or profile.

Whether your policy obliges the insurer to do anything about that depends on
your policy's wording on matching and uniform appearance. It varies, genuinely,
and anyone who tells you Texas has a blanket matching rule is overstating it.
What you can do is pin the question down: ask your insurer, in writing, how the
policy handles a repair that cannot be matched, and get the answer in writing
too.

### "They approved a repair and the roof needs replacing"

This is a disagreement about scope, and it is the most winnable kind, because
it turns on evidence rather than interpretation. Hail bruising scattered across
every elevation and wind creasing over large areas are
[field failures, not detail failures](/blog/repair-or-replace-your-roof/) — no
amount of patching catches up when the material itself is finished.

## What you can actually do about a shortfall

**Ask for the adjuster's report and read the scope.** You are entitled to see
what was approved, line by line. Very often the shortfall is one or two missing
line items — ridge cap, drip edge, underlayment, ventilation — rather than a
dispute about the roof.

**Request a reinspection with your documentation.** Not a phone argument: a
second look with slope-by-slope photographs in front of them. A decision made
without evidence can be remade with it.

**Check the clock.** Texas Insurance Code Chapter 542 sets deadlines on
insurers — acknowledge and begin investigating within 15 days, accept or reject
within 15 business days of receiving everything they asked for, pay within 5
business days of acceptance, with a catastrophe extension available. Delay
beyond those windows carries consequences for them, not you.

**Use the appraisal clause if you're still apart.** Most Texas policies contain
one. Each side hires an appraiser, the two appraisers choose an umpire, and the
amount gets decided outside of litigation.
[A denial usually isn't the end of it](/blog/roof-claim-denied-texas-appraisal/)
covers how that runs and what it can and cannot fix — it settles the _amount_
of a covered loss, not whether the loss is covered at all.

**Know who can negotiate for you, and who can't.** Under Insurance Code
§4102.163 your contractor cannot act as a public insurance adjuster on your
claim. We can document, meet your adjuster on the roof and argue the correct
scope with evidence. We cannot negotiate your settlement, and a roofer who
offers to is either unlicensed or unclear about which hat they're wearing.

## And if the gap is just real

Sometimes the claim is correct, the scope is right, and there's still a gap
between the settlement and the roof you want — usually because you're upgrading,
or because an older roof's depreciation was always going to be steep.

That's a budgeting question rather than a claims question.
[How people actually pay for a new roof](/blog/how-to-pay-for-a-new-roof-texas/)
sets out the routes, and [financing options](/financing) lists the lenders
without us pretending to be one. What we won't do is quietly reduce the scope
to hit the cheque and leave you with a roof that's missing the parts nobody
sees.
