You're getting a house in The Woodlands ready to list, and the roof is the line
item you keep circling. Replace it and eat five figures? Patch it? Price around
it? Here's the honest framework we give sellers and the agents who bring us in —
including the data that says a new roof usually doesn't pay for itself, and the
cheaper move that often works better.

## What buyers and inspectors actually see

Buyers see the roof twice: once from the curb, and once through their
inspector's report. Both matter.

**From the curb:** dark algae streaking is the big one here. It's cosmetic — the
Gulf-states algae _Gloeocapsa magma_ — but a streaked roof reads as "old roof"
to a buyer before they've stepped out of the car. University of Georgia
Extension notes humid-area roofs often darken within 5–7 years, heaviest on
shaded slopes — which describes half the streets in
[Panther Creek](/the-woodlands/panther-creek) and Cochran's Crossing. One
caution before you fix it: never power-wash asphalt shingles — ARMA warns
against it flatly, because blasting off granules shortens the roof's life.
Proper chemical cleaning or simply disclosing the streaking honestly both beat
a pressure washer.

**From the report:** inspectors date the roof and flag its weak points. The
InterNACHI life-expectancy chart most inspectors reference puts 3-tab shingles
at about 20 years and architectural shingles at about 30 — with the pointed
caveat that **"hot climates drastically reduce asphalt shingle life."** They'll
also check the usual leak suspects: pipe boots, whose neoprene collars degrade
in UV and commonly fail within roughly 10–15 years — one of the most common
causes of roof leaks — plus flashing, and any soft decking. A home built in the
mid-1990s in [Alden Bridge](/the-woodlands/alden-bridge) that's still on its
original roof is going to draw exactly this scrutiny.

An aging-but-sound roof is a negotiation item. An _actively leaking_ roof is a
deal risk: it spooks buyers and invites renegotiation at the worst possible
moment.

## The recoup math: a new roof rarely pays 100 cents on the dollar

The _Remodeling_ Cost vs. Value report — the standard industry reference —
tracks what an asphalt-shingle roof replacement returns at resale nationally.
Its recent national figures have been widely reported at under 60% recoup
(roughly 57 cents on the dollar in the 2024 report, as secondary sources cite
it). Treat the exact percentage loosely, but the direction is consistent:
**replacing a roof purely to sell usually returns most — not all — of its cost.**

So why does anyone do it? Because recoup percentage isn't the whole equation.
A failed roof can cost you buyers, time on market, and financing hiccups — none
of which show up in that number. The honest framing:

- **Roof is old but sound, no active leaks:** replacement is usually _not_ the
  best money. Consider pricing transparently or offering a credit.
- **Roof is failing or leaking:** fix it before listing. Whether that's repair
  or replacement depends on the scope — see below.
- **Everything else about the house is dialed and the roof is the one eyesore:**
  a new roof can strengthen the listing, especially in a competitive village.
  Just go in knowing the data says you're buying marketability, not profit.

## Repair vs. replace: mind the gap

The price gap between the two options is enormous. According to Angi (2026),
typical roof repairs run **$400–$2,000**, while the national average roof
replacement is **$9,602** (with a full range of $5,900–$46,000). If the real
problems are a handful of failed pipe boots, some wind-lifted shingles, and one
flashing detail, [a targeted repair](/services/roof-repair) plus documentation
can get a mid-life roof through inspection for a fraction of replacement cost.

What doesn't work: cosmetic cover-ups. And in The Woodlands, remember that if
you _do_ replace, the Township requires prior written approval from your
village's design review committee before the roof goes on — build that into
your listing timeline.

## Disclose it straight

The seller's disclosure notice is not the place to get creative about the roof. If it leaked and was repaired — disclose it, with the
invoice. If there's a known issue you're pricing around — disclose it.
Undisclosed roof problems have a way of resurfacing after closing, angrier and
more expensive. A documented, honestly disclosed roof — even an imperfect one —
sells better than a mystery.

## Bottom line

Don't reflexively buy a new roof to sell a house — the recoup data doesn't
support it as a default. Get the roof inspected, fix what's actually broken,
document everything, and disclose it straight. Replace only when the roof is
truly at end-of-life or failing — and then do it properly, with Township
approval handled.
