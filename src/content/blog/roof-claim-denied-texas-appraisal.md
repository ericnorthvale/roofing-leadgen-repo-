A denial letter reads like the end of the conversation. It usually isn't.

Two of the roofs we have replaced in the past year started with a denied claim.
One was denied because the homeowner's previous contractor didn't show up to
the adjuster meeting. One was denied outright and reopened through a process
most homeowners have never heard of. Both ended in a full approved replacement.

Here is what actually happens after a denial in Texas, in the order it tends to
happen, and — just as importantly — what each step cannot do for you.

## First, work out which kind of "no" you got

This is the single most useful distinction in the whole process, and almost
nobody explains it.

- **"We don't think there's enough damage to exceed your deductible."** That is
  a disagreement about the **amount of loss**.
- **"This damage isn't covered — your roof is worn out, not storm-damaged."**
  That is a disagreement about **coverage**.

They look identical in a letter. They are resolved by completely different
routes, and the tool everybody reaches for first only works on one of them.

## Step one: ask for a reinspection

Often the cheapest fix is the simplest. An adjuster spent a limited amount of
time on your roof, possibly in poor light, possibly without getting on the
steep slopes. If the inspection was thin, you can request a reinspection.

**This is the one that fixed our Woodlands job.** The homeowner's first
contractor missed the adjuster meeting entirely, so nobody was there to point
anything out, and the claim was denied. We re-inspected, requested a
reinspection, and met the adjuster on the roof ourselves with the hail damage
documented. It came back as a full roof replacement approval, and the homeowner
paid what their policy required and nothing more.

What makes a reinspection work is not arguing. It is having photographs of
specific, located damage, taken before anyone touched the roof — which is why
we shoot a forty-photo packet on every storm inspection whether or not a claim
ever gets filed.

## Step two: the appraisal clause — and it's new for 2026

If the disagreement is about **how much**, your policy almost certainly now
contains an appraisal provision. **Senate Bill 458 requires Texas residential
property policies issued or renewed on or after 1 January 2026 to include
one**, and the Texas Department of Insurance has adopted rules implementing it
(28 TAC §§5.9800–5.9806).

How it works: each side appoints an appraiser. The two appraisers try to agree
on the amount of loss. If they can't, they select an umpire; if they can't agree
on an umpire either, a county or district court can appoint one. The resulting
award is binding as to the amount of loss, barring fraud, accident or material
mistake.

**This is what reopened our Spring job.** An aging roof began leaking after a
major renovation, we identified covered storm damage, the claim was initially
denied, and we pursued it through appraisal to an approved full replacement —
managing the process start to finish so the homeowner didn't have to.

### What appraisal can't do

Appraisal determines the amount of loss. It does not decide whether something
is covered at all — the Texas Insurance Code is explicit that it is a process
"solely to determine the amount of loss" (§1813.003(b)).

So if your carrier's position is "this is wear and tear, not hail," you are in a
coverage fight, and invoking appraisal does not resolve it on its own.

There is one important wrinkle in your favour, though. In *State Farm Lloyds v.
Johnson* (Tex. 2009), the Texas Supreme Court held that appraisers **may**
decide how much of the roof damage was caused by hail versus wear and tear,
because that question goes to the amount of loss. The line between the two
disputes is blurrier than carriers sometimes suggest.

## Step three: the clock your insurer is on

Texas prompt-payment law puts your carrier on deadlines, and most homeowners
never find out they exist:

- **Acknowledge** your claim within 15 days of notice.
- **Accept or reject** within 15 business days of receiving everything they
  asked for — with one 45-day extension if they give you written reasons.
- **Pay** within 5 business days of accepting.
- Extensions apply in a declared catastrophe, and statutory penalties start
  past 60 days.

If your claim has gone quiet, that timeline is worth knowing by heart.

## Two things we will not do, whatever the outcome

**We are not a public adjuster.** Texas Insurance Code §4102.163 prohibits a
roofer from adjusting a claim on the same property. We document the damage and
provide a scope; a separately licensed public adjuster, if you hire one,
negotiates the claim. Anyone blurring that line is doing something they are not
allowed to do.

**We will not waive or absorb your deductible.** Texas law requires you to pay
it (§707.002) and makes it an offence for a contractor to pay, waive or rebate
it (Business & Commerce Code §27.02). "We'll eat your deductible" is not
generosity — it is insurance fraud with you as a participant.
