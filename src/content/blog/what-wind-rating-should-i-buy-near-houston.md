Shingle packaging is a fog of class letters and mile-per-hour numbers, and most
of it goes unexplained at the kitchen table. Here's what the ratings actually
mean, what wind our storms actually deliver north of Houston, and how to buy a
roof that's built for the next storm, not the last one.

## Decoding the ratings on the wrapper

**ASTM D3161** is a fan-driven wind test lasting two hours. Class A means the
shingle held at 60 mph, Class D at 90 mph, and **Class F at 110 mph** — the top
tier, and what you should expect from any serious architectural shingle sold
here.

**ASTM D7158** is a newer uplift-based method with Classes D, G, and H —
**Class H is the highest**. The residential code (IRC Table R905.2.6.1) maps
your area's design wind speed to the class of shingle allowed, which is why
these letters aren't just marketing — they're a code-compliance item.

**UL 790** also shows up next to these, but don't confuse it: that's the _fire_
rating (Class A is best), not wind.

The good news: the IKO shingles we install — Dynasty and the Class 4 Nordic —
back their wind resistance with a **130-mph limited high-wind warranty** and the
reinforced ArmourZone nailing band that resists uplift. Once you're at that level,
the class letters stop being the differentiator, and the warranty conditions and
installation become the whole game.

## What our storms actually deliver

Ratings only matter measured against reality, so here's the local record:

- The NWS counts **50–60 thunderstorm days per year** in Southeast Texas —
  roughly a third of them severe — with **downbursts capable of 60–100+ mph**.
  That's the routine threat, not the headline hurricane.
- **Hurricane Beryl (July 8, 2024)** made landfall near Matagorda as a Category
  1 with 80-mph winds; measured gusts reached **69 mph at Conroe**, 83 mph at
  Intercontinental, and 84 mph at Hobby (NWS Houston/Galveston).
- The **May 2024 derecho** was the sobering one: NWS surveys estimated winds
  around **100 mph through parts of downtown Houston** — thunderstorm winds, no
  hurricane required. (That was a Houston-metro event; the official storm
  database doesn't record those extremes up in Montgomery County that day.)
- Closer to home, NCEI's Storm Events database logged estimated thunderstorm
  winds of roughly **75–78 mph in southern Montgomery County on May 26, 2025**.

So the realistic design case near Houston is repeated 60–100+ mph thunderstorm
wind over the life of the roof, with an occasional tropical system on top.
A 60-mph-class shingle has no business here; even the 110-mph D3161 Class F
test is a two-hour lab snapshot, not a 20-year guarantee.

## The part nobody puts on the wrapper: the seal

IBHS research is blunt about wind mechanics: **sealant bond strength is the key
factor** in whether shingles stay on. Wind failure starts with uplift — the
shingle lifts, creases, or tears — and IBHS also found that shingle wind
performance **declines with age**. Two consequences for buyers:

1. A top-rated shingle installed badly — nails high, seal strips contaminated,
   cold-weather install that never bonded — performs like a bad shingle.
2. A 17-year-old roof that "still looks fine" is measurably weaker against the
   next derecho than it was against the last one. If yours took a hit, get it
   looked at — that's what our [storm damage service](/the-woodlands/storm-damage)
   exists for.

## Wind _warranties_: the number that's actually enforceable

Lab classes are pass/fail tests. Warranties are promises with conditions, and
the conditions are where buyers get burned. Here's the coverage on the IKO
shingles we install:

- **IKO Dynasty & Nordic:** a **130-mph limited high-wind warranty** — but only
  when the shingles are installed to IKO's application requirements, which lean
  hard on the reinforced ArmourZone nailing band and a properly sealed starter
  course at the edges.
- **The edges matter as much as the field.** Most wind failures start at the
  eaves and rakes, so a purpose-made starter strip (IKO Leading Edge Plus) sealing
  those wind-driven zones is part of earning the coverage, not an upsell.

Notice the pattern: the shingle alone isn't the warranty — the _system and the
installation method_ are. Make your contract name the warranty and the components
and application spec that earn it. (For a fuller comparison of the two IKO lines,
see [Dynasty vs. Nordic](/blog/iko-dynasty-vs-nordic).)

## Nailing patterns: cheap insurance

Fastening is the other half of wind resistance. Standard application is typically
four nails per shingle; high-wind specs call for enhanced fastening per the
manufacturer's application instructions. Some local authorities
mandate it outright — the City of Shenandoah's published residential roofing
rules require a **six-nail pattern** rated for 110-mph three-second gusts, with
ASTM D3161 Class F or G shingles. Six nails per shingle costs little on a full
replacement, and we think Shenandoah has the right idea for the whole area.
