You have three quotes on the kitchen table. One says $11,400. One says $16,900.
One says $9,200. They are all for "a new roof."

They are not comparable, and the spread almost never means what people assume
it means. It usually means the three roofers scoped three different jobs — and
the cheapest one is cheapest because it left things out that you will pay for
later, out of your own pocket, after the crew has gone.

Here is what a real estimate contains. Use it on us too.

## 1. A measured roof, in squares

A square is 100 square feet of roof surface. Every honest quote states how many
squares it covers, because that number drives everything else: material,
labour, waste, disposal.

If a roofer quoted you without measuring — no ladder, no drone, no report —
they estimated your roof from the driveway and built margin in to cover being
wrong. You can check their number yourself; the
[roof size calculator](/roof-size-calculator) will get you close in a couple of
minutes.

## 2. The pitch and the number of layers coming off

Steeper roofs cost more to work on. So do roofs with two or three existing
layers, because tear-off and disposal double or triple.

A quote that doesn't mention either is a quote that may be about to discover
them.

## 3. A per-sheet price for decking — **before** the tear-off

This is the one that matters most, and the one most often missing.

Nobody — not us, not anyone — knows how much of your plywood decking is rotten
until the old roof is off. That's not evasion; the decking is underneath
everything, which is exactly why
[a roof can look serviceable and be sitting on soft plywood](/blog/repair-or-replace-your-roof/).

So the honest way to handle it is to name the price per sheet in advance.
Then, if we find eight bad sheets, the cost is arithmetic you already agreed
to rather than a conversation on day two when you have no leverage and half a
roof.

**A replacement quote that doesn't mention decking at all is not a cheaper
quote. It's an incomplete one.**

## 4. The actual shingle — brand, line and colour

Not "architectural shingles." Not "30-year dimensional."

Brand, product line, colour. _IKO Dynasty in Driftshake._ There is a real
difference between shingle lines in impact rating, wind rating and warranty,
and "architectural" covers all of them. If the line isn't named, you cannot
compare two quotes and you cannot check what you were given afterwards.

The [design centre](/roof-design-center) shows what the lines actually look
like, and [Dynasty vs Nordic](/blog/iko-dynasty-vs-nordic/) explains what the
upgrade buys.

## 5. Underlayment, and which kind

Felt and synthetic are not the same product and do not cost the same. The quote
should say which is going on your roof.

## 6. Ice-and-water shield at the valleys and penetrations

Valleys carry the most water on a roof, so they fail first. Self-adhered
membrane in the valleys and around penetrations is standard practice — and a
standard place to save money quietly.

## 7. Drip edge

Metal edging at eaves and rakes. It's required by code, it's cheap, and it is
routinely omitted from low bids because almost no homeowner checks.

## 8. Flashing — replaced, not reused

Step flashing at walls, counter-flashing at chimneys, pipe boots at every
penetration.

Reusing old pipe boots on a new roof is the single most common corner cut in
this trade. The neoprene collar cracks with UV exposure, commonly within 10 to
15 years, and it is behind more mystery ceiling stains than anything else on a
roof — [we wrote a whole post on it](/blog/why-roofs-leak-around-pipe-boots/).
Putting a fifteen-year-old boot back on a brand-new roof is building in a leak
and charging you for a new roof.

The quote should say **new**.

## 9. Ventilation — what's there and what's changing

Ridge vents, turbines, soffit intake. If your attic was under-ventilated
before, a new roof over the same bad ventilation gets you the same early
failure again, on your money this time.

[Attic ventilation is why Texas roofs die early](/blog/attic-ventilation-texas-roof-lifespan/)
covers the 1-in-300 benchmark. An estimate should at minimum tell you whether
yours meets it.

## 10. Two warranties, stated separately

There are always two, and conflating them is how people end up with neither.

- **Manufacturer warranty** covers the shingles, and its tier depends on the
  installer's certification level and what else from the system was installed.
- **Workmanship warranty** covers the installation, and it is only worth the
  company standing behind it.

The quote should state both, by name and length.
[How roof warranties actually work](/warranty) sets out the tiers.

## 11. Who pulls the permit and files the HOA submittal

In most of our area — unincorporated Harris and Montgomery County — a re-roof
needs no building permit, and your HOA or architectural-review committee
governs instead. Inside the City of Conroe, Houston or Tomball, a permit does
apply.

Either way: **the quote should say who is doing it.** "You'll need to sort out
your HOA" discovered after the material is ordered in a non-approved colour is
an expensive sentence.

## 12. A payment schedule — and what Texas law says about deposits

A deposit schedule is normal. What is not normal is a large cash payment up
front, before any material or crew appears.

The Texas Department of Insurance is blunt about this after a disaster: it is
illegal for an out-of-town contractor to take a down payment before starting
work, and you should never sign a contract that assigns the work "for the value
of insurance proceeds."

Two more things that follow from Texas law, and that a legitimate estimate will
never offer you:

**Nobody can waive your deductible.** Under Texas Insurance Code §707.002 the
insured _shall_ pay the deductible, and Business &amp; Commerce Code §27.02
makes it an offence for a contractor to pay, rebate or absorb it. The Texas
Department of Insurance notes it carries up to a $2,000 fine and up to six
months in jail. A roofer who offers to "eat your deductible" is proposing
insurance fraud and naming you as the other party to it.

**Your roofer is not your public adjuster.** Under Insurance Code §4102.163 a
contractor cannot act as a public insurance adjuster on your claim. We can
document damage, meet your adjuster on the roof and advocate for the correct
scope — we cannot negotiate your settlement on your behalf, and anyone offering
to is either unlicensed or confused about which hat they're wearing.

## The three-bid problem, honestly

TDI recommends getting three bids, and that's good advice — but only if the
three are scoped the same.

What usually happens instead is that the low bid omits decking contingency,
reuses flashing and pipe boots, uses felt instead of synthetic, skips drip
edge, and names no shingle line. It is genuinely cheaper, because it is
genuinely less. Then the decking surprise arrives mid-job, and the final
number lands above the quote you turned down.

So the useful question isn't "who's cheapest." It's **"are these quotes for the
same work?"** — and the only way to answer it is line by line.
