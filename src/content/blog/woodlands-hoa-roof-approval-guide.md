In The Woodlands, you don't pull a county permit to replace your roof — you
get covenant approval. Skip it and you're not dodging paperwork; you're
starting a compliance problem on a recorded covenant that runs with your deed.
The good news: the process is written down, the rules are reasonable, and once
you know the sequence it's genuinely manageable. Here's the whole thing, step
by step, straight from the Township's 2024 Residential Development Standards
(recorded December 10, 2024).

## Who actually approves your roof

Two bodies matter, and homeowners regularly mix them up:

- **Your village's Residential Design Review Committee (RDRC).** Each
  developed village has one — a committee of at least three elected resident
  members that meets at least monthly. This is who reviews your roof
  application. The committees are staffed and supported by the Township's
  **Covenant Administration Department** (281-210-3800, 2801 Technology Forest
  Blvd), which is your practical point of contact for questions and status.
- **The Development Standards Committee (DSC).** A seven-member committee that
  adopts and enforces the Residential Development Standards — and the only
  committee with the power to grant variances. You'll deal with the DSC only
  if you appeal or need something outside the standards.

(Bit of history for newer residents: covenant enforcement moved from the
original homeowner associations to the Township in 2010 — so yes, "the
Township" is effectively your HOA for design review.)

## Step 1: Know what needs approval

The standards are blunt. Section 1.5(I)(A) requires prior written approval for
**"All roof replacement or house repainting."** Every replacement, every
village, every time — same-for-same shingle color included.

Repairs aren't exempt either. The minor-repair exception in §1.5(I)(B)
specifically excludes "fencing, roofing and color changes, which require a
permit at the time of repair or replacement." So a repair that involves
roofing materials needs a permit at the time of the work — worth knowing
before a storm-chaser nails a patch on and disappears. (Our
[Woodlands roof repair](/the-woodlands/roof-repair) jobs include this step.)

A short list of items is **pre-approved with no application at all**: gutters,
downspouts, and roof stacks or vents in a color compatible with the home
(§1.6). And roof work involving vents or stacks should put them on rear
slopes where possible, with exposed roof accessories matching or compatible
with the roof color (§1.6(H)). Solar collectors have their own rule:
flat-profile, conforming to the roof slope, painted to match (§1.6(J)).

## Step 2: Pick a material and color that pass

Section 1.6(H)(4) sets the palette. Acceptable roofing materials:

- Tile
- Slate
- Composition shingles
- Stone-coated or standing-seam metal (free of glossy finish)
- Cement-fiberboard

Colors must harmonize with the natural landscape — **"muted in shades and
without color pattern."** In practice that's the earth-toned, non-flashy end
of every manufacturer's line, which covers most architectural shingles people
actually want. What it rules out are the bright, high-contrast, or patterned
looks. The committee may require **product samples** (§1.5(II)(D)), so have
the shingle name, manufacturer, and a sample board ready rather than "brown."

One helpful absence: **no property survey is required** for a re-roof
application (§1.5(II)(E)). That removes the most annoying document from the
usual HOA pile.

## Step 3: Apply through the Civic Access Portal

Applications go through the Township's **Civic Access Portal**, its online
permitting system. Straightforward, standards-conforming applications can move
through **staff approval** rather than waiting for a committee meeting; the
standards also provide a **pre-approval** path. Committee review is the
default for anything else.

## Step 4: Respect the 45-day clock

The committee has a **45-day review window** — and here's the trap: if no
action is taken within 45 days, the application is **automatically
disapproved** (§1.5(III)(A),(B)(4)). Silence is a "no," not a "yes." An
application that stalls on a missing sample or an ambiguous color name doesn't
drift through by default; it dies. This is the single best argument for
submitting a complete, sample-backed application the first time.

## Step 5 (if needed): Appeal

Disapproved? You can appeal **in writing within 10 days** (§1.5(III)(C)).
Appeals go up to the DSC — which, remember, is the only body that can grant a
variance from the standards. Most roof applications never get here; the ones
that do usually involve an unusual material or color, which is exactly what
variance power exists for.

Also worth knowing: if the standards ever conflict with the building code,
**the code prevails** (§1.6(G)). Aesthetics never override safety
requirements.

## The Grogan's Forest exception

Homes in **Grogan's Forest Sections 5, 6 and 7** submit roof applications to
the **City of Shenandoah**, not the Township — a genuine quirk documented on
the Township's permitting-process page, and Shenandoah runs a full municipal
permit program with its own material and inspection rules. If that's your
section, our [Montgomery County permit guide](/blog/montgomery-county-roofing-permit-guide)
covers the Shenandoah side in detail.

## After approval: the covenant keeps running

Approval isn't the end of your roof's covenant life. The maintenance standards
(§3.2) require owners to keep roofs repaired and clear of "leaves, pine
needles, branches, etc." — no small thing under a Woodlands tree canopy. A
roof shedding pine needles into every valley is both a covenant issue and a
genuine maintenance one, which is part of why we push regular inspections in
[The Woodlands](/the-woodlands) harder than anywhere else we work.
