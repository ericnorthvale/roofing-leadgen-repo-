Those dark streaks running down your north-facing slope are not dirt, not
mould, and not a sign the roof is failing.

They are a living organism — **Gloeocapsa magma**, a cyanobacterium — and under
our humidity it is less a question of whether you'll get it than when.

## What it actually is

Gloeocapsa magma is airborne, lands on a roof, and does well anywhere that
stays damp. It protects itself from UV by producing a dark pigmented sheath,
which is the black you can see. The streaks run _downward_ because rain carries
the colonies down the slope.

It shows up first on north-facing slopes and under tree cover for the same
reason: those surfaces stay wet longest after it rains. Around here, with
pine canopy over a lot of the housing stock and humidity that rarely lets up,
that is most roofs eventually.

## Is it damaging the roof?

Mostly it is cosmetic — but not entirely.

The practical harm is that a dark colony holds moisture against the shingle and
absorbs more heat than the surrounding surface. Neither is good for an asphalt
shingle in a climate that is already hard on them. It is not an emergency, and
it is not a reason to replace a roof, but "purely harmless" overstates it.

What it definitely does is make a sound roof look neglected, which matters if
you're selling.

## How to clean it — and the mistake that ruins roofs

The Asphalt Roofing Manufacturers Association — the manufacturers' own trade
body — is clear on this, and most homeowners do the opposite.

**Do not pressure wash an asphalt roof.** ARMA's guidance states plainly that
pressure washers cause granule loss and probable early failure of the roof.
Granules are the shingle's UV armour. Blast them off and you have traded a
cosmetic problem for a structural one, and done in an afternoon what a decade
of weather would have done.

What ARMA points to instead is a **low-pressure (soft wash) application** of a
**50/50 mix of household-strength sodium hypochlorite and water**, without
mechanical cleaning equipment. Wet surrounding plants before and rinse them
after — the solution will burn foliage.

One caveat worth checking: your specific shingle manufacturer's cleaning
instructions can differ from the general guidance, and they are what your
warranty is written against.

Honestly, this is also a job worth handing to a soft-wash company rather than
doing yourself. Walking a wet roof covered in bleach solution is exactly as
dangerous as it sounds.

## The truth about zinc and copper strips

Here is where we differ from a lot of what you'll be told.

Zinc and copper are genuinely algae-inhibiting — rain carries trace metal down
the slope and suppresses growth below the strip. That part is real, and it is
why you sometimes see a clean band under a copper chimney flashing.

But **ARMA presents these strips as something to consider when a roof is
replaced — not as a retrofit to an existing one.** The reason is mechanical:
retrofitting a strip to a finished roof means either exposed nails, which are a
leak risk, or breaking the sealant bond between shingle courses, which can
cause wind damage later.

So when somebody offers to fit zinc strips to your existing roof as an add-on,
they're proposing something the manufacturers' own association flags as
problematic. We'd rather tell you that than sell it to you.

## What we actually recommend

**If the roof is sound and you want it cleaner:** a soft wash, done properly,
by someone insured to be up there. Expect it to come back eventually; this is
maintenance, not a cure.

**If the roof is nearing replacement anyway:** don't spend money cleaning it.
Put it toward the new roof, and get algae-resistant shingles.

**At replacement:** the IKO shingles we install carry a **10-year
algae-resistance warranty**, and that's the point at which copper or zinc
strips can be integrated properly rather than bolted on. Addressing it then
costs almost nothing extra and avoids the retrofit problem entirely.

## One thing it is worth ruling out

Algae streaks are not the same as **granule loss**, though people confuse them.
Streaking is dark colour sitting on top of the shingle. Granule loss is the
shingle's surface wearing away, exposing the asphalt underneath — that one does
mean the roof is ageing out, and it's one of the things we check on a
[free roof inspection](/services/roof-inspection).

If you're not sure which you're looking at, that's a sensible reason to have
